# Reading Base Editing Standards by Level

Use this guide when designing, revising, or evaluating Reading Base stories. The standards were distilled from approved stories and revision decisions in the Reading Base Level 1-4 working notebooks. Keep the reusable decisions; do not copy raw chat or unverified drafts into the final reader.

## Authority Order

Before writing, confirm the target level and unit. Use sources in this order:

1. official EOW scope, vocabulary, grammar, Reading, and Value for the selected level and unit;
2. the approved Reading Base plan or Canva master for that level and unit;
3. the level-specific standards below;
4. approved prior Reading Base stories as style examples.

If a required source is missing or conflicts with another source, mark it as `DATA NEEDED`. Do not solve a source conflict by inventing content.

## Story Count and Alignment

Do not assume that every level uses the same number of stories, page count, or activity format. Verify the approved plan for the selected level and unit first.

When a unit uses the four-story structure, follow this alignment:

| Story | Required alignment |
|---|---|
| Story 1 | Vocabulary 1 + Grammar 1 |
| Story 2 | Vocabulary 2 + Grammar 2 |
| Story 3 | The unit's Reading section |
| Story 4 | The unit's Value section |

When an approved plan uses three stories, serial chapters, or a story-to-workshop sequence, preserve that structure. Record the source alignment of every story or chapter instead of forcing the four-story formula onto it.

## Standards Shared by All Levels

- Stay inside the selected level and unit. Prior-level language may be used for spiral review, but it must be familiar and should be recorded as review language.
- Build the story around one clear child-interest hook, real-world question, problem, surprise, or age-appropriate classic adaptation.
- Preserve story logic. Time, place, character motivation, point of view, and cause-and-effect must remain consistent.
- Integrate target language through actions and situations. Do not add unnatural questions or dialogue only to display a grammar pattern.
- Let humor come from a situation, visual surprise, character choice, or reversal. Do not make a culture, identity, appearance, disability, or unfamiliar custom the joke.
- Verify cultural and factual details. Use respectful, observable descriptions instead of stereotypes.
- Match emotional reactions to the situation. When a child is upset, an adult should normally acknowledge the feeling before teaching or correcting.
- Give the ending a payoff: solve the opening problem, reveal the surprise, complete the classic-story turn, or show the Value through changed behavior.
- Make the activity test comprehension of the actual story while reusing the target language.
- Treat NotebookLM output as a draft. A human-approved version or clearly labeled final note is stronger evidence than an earlier model suggestion.

## Level 1

### Reader Experience

- Write for emerging readers who rely heavily on pictures, repetition, and familiar sentence frames.
- A useful observed format is a cover plus four story pages, with an upper and lower scene on each page. Confirm the actual Canva template before finalizing.
- Give each panel one visually obvious action or surprise. The picture should make the sentence easier to understand.
- Use short, direct chunks and controlled repetition. Keep names easy to pronounce.

### Language

- Use the target unit's vocabulary and grammar first, then familiar language from earlier Level 1 units.
- Prefer familiar words over abstract explanations or culture-specific terminology when the terminology is not part of the learning goal.
- Model complete short answers when the grammar requires them, such as `No, it isn't` or `No, they aren't`.
- Keep the point of view consistent. If the child narrates with `I` and `my`, do not switch to `he`, `his`, or an outside narrator without a clear reason.
- A direct statement is often more natural than a repeated question. Do not ask `What is she wearing?` when the scene works better as `She is wearing a beautiful pink dress.`

### Story and Humor

- Strong options include toy confusion, animals, family or school situations, visual mix-ups, repeated surprises, and gentle slapstick.
- Keep the comedy warm. Avoid labels such as `silly` when a more affectionate reaction such as `Oh, Robby!` fits the relationship better.
- Cultural stories should invite curiosity and respect. Traditional clothing, for example, should be described through visible colors, shapes, patterns, movement, and function—not treated as a costume disaster.
- A classic adaptation should keep the original story's essential turn. Simplify the language without removing the reason the characters act as they do.

### Activity

- Use image-supported matching, circling, sequencing, or short multiple-choice comprehension.
- Recycle the target singular/plural and question/answer patterns, but ensure the correct answer depends on reading the story or picture.

## Level 2

### Reader Experience

- Give the story a clearer beginning, problem, consequence, repair, and ending than Level 1.
- An observed working format is a cover, a longer page-by-page story sequence, and a final activity page. Confirm the exact page count in the approved template.
- Use real-world wonders and logical mismatches as humor: daylight at bedtime, a missed bus, or an everyday plan going wrong.

### Language

- Maintain short, accessible sentences while allowing more connected narration and emotional sequence.
- Use full grammatical responses rather than isolated `Yes` or `No`.
- Spiral-review Level 1 rooms, emotions, actions, transportation, furniture, and prepositions when they support the plot.
- Replace abstract phrases such as `prepare in advance` with language children already know, such as `Let's plan your day.`

### Story Logic and Value

- Check practical logic carefully. A child on vacation should not suddenly prepare for school unless the story establishes why.
- Use one consistent point of view. First person can strengthen a search, mistake, or consequence story.
- Show a real consequence before teaching the Value. Missing the bus, feeling sad, receiving comfort, making a plan, and succeeding the next day is stronger than a lecture.
- Let adults respond with empathy first and guidance second: comfort, name the feeling, then offer a simple next action.
- Turn the Value into a concrete behavior the child can copy, such as packing a bag, placing shoes near the door, or following a bedtime checklist.

### Activity

- Use comprehension questions, sequencing, complete-answer practice, or a small real-life Value checklist.
- The activity should help children transfer the story lesson to their own routine.

## Level 3

### Reader Experience

- Avoid nursery-level songs, overly cute plots, and conflicts that feel too simple for older elementary learners.
- Use stronger stakes and agency: a race, mystery, chase, competition, map puzzle, performance, or multi-step mission.
- Check that the central idea is not a repeat of an earlier story. Change the story engine, not only the character names or setting.
- A serial chapter structure can work when the Reading Base story supports a multi-day course or camp.

### Language and Tone

- Keep grammar tense under control and document the allowed tense range before drafting.
- Give characters goals, decisions, setbacks, and strategic choices. Older learners should feel that the characters are doing more than identifying vocabulary.
- Use age-appropriate rhythm, chant, or rap when it improves participation; do not default to a preschool song.
- Connect topics to the learners' own lives: daily routines, dream jobs, map solving, transportation choices, comparisons, and teamwork.

### Story-to-Workshop Design

- The story remains the hook and mission briefing. It must explain why the class will do the following game, challenge, or Workshop.
- Derive the Workshop from the story's unresolved task. Do not write an unrelated story after the activity has already been chosen.
- Make the activity require meaningful speaking, decision-making, comparison, navigation, or collaboration—not only recall.
- A final chapter can hand the mission directly to the students, for example by revealing a board-game challenge that the class must complete.

## Level 4

### Reader Experience

- Use mature but accessible stories built around real problems, knowledge, investigation, social observation, or emotionally credible relationships.
- In the reviewed Level 4 Unit 5 plan, three stories were approved and each served a different function. Do not add a fourth story unless the official unit plan requires one.
- An observed working target is roughly four pages and 400-450 words per story. Treat this as a reference, not a universal template; verify the current design.

### Language and Narrative Depth

- Use natural dialogue. A sentence can be grammatically correct but still sound like restaurant ordering, a lecture, or a model exercise in the wrong setting.
- Let vocabulary and grammar solve the story's problem. Avoid listing every target item when it makes the prose mechanical.
- Include sensory details, character gestures, and specific observations, but keep one central problem. Added detail should deepen the story, not make the plot harder to follow.
- Higher-level ideas such as local food, food miles, rooftop farming, or natural pest control can be used when facts are verified and explained through the story.
- Give learners room for inference, comparison, and critical thinking instead of explaining every conclusion for them.

### Variety Across a Unit

- Deliberately separate the stories by genre, emotional tone, setting, and character role.
- A useful three-story contrast is:
  1. a school or science challenge with strategic problem-solving;
  2. a warm family story with an emotional payoff;
  3. an investigation or social-observation story that reaches an evidence-based conclusion.
- If two ideas use the same emotional engine—for example, helping an older family member solve a garden problem—redesign one before drafting.
- Prefer a focused, memorable premise over unnecessary complications. Simplifying the plot does not mean flattening the characters or removing meaningful details.

### Activity

- Ask learners to infer, compare evidence, explain a decision, sequence a process, or apply the story's real-world idea.
- Preserve natural language use. The activity should not turn a thoughtful story back into a mechanical vocabulary list.

## Common Revision Decisions

| Weak draft signal | Preferred revision |
|---|---|
| The same lower-level formula is used for every level | Reconfirm the level, reader age, structure, and approved template |
| Grammar is inserted as unnatural question-and-answer dialogue | Use narration or direct description when it sounds more natural |
| A cultural difference is used as the joke | Use respectful observation, beauty, movement, pattern, and function |
| The plot contradicts its own setting or timeline | Repair the causal chain before polishing sentences |
| An adult corrects an upset child immediately | Show empathy first, then a simple action the child can take |
| Advanced wording explains a simple Value | Replace it with familiar words and demonstrate the Value through action |
| Several stories in one unit feel emotionally identical | Assign each story a different genre, setting, conflict, and payoff |
| A Level 3 story feels childish or low-stakes | Add agency, challenge, strategy, rhythm, or a meaningful mission |
| A Level 4 story accumulates too many mechanisms | Return to one central problem and keep only details that deepen it |
| A Workshop exists without a story reason | Make the story deliver the mission that launches the Workshop |

## Drafting and Approval Workflow

1. Confirm level, unit, official sources, story count, page template, and story alignment.
2. List required new language and allowed spiral-review language.
3. Assign a distinct story function, genre, emotional tone, and learning payoff.
4. Write the plot logic in Chinese before drafting the English text.
5. Draft page by page with picture action, story text, and teaching purpose aligned.
6. Audit vocabulary difficulty, grammar accuracy, point of view, timeline, motivation, cultural accuracy, and naturalness.
7. Design an activity that depends on understanding the story.
8. Compare the story with the other stories in the unit to remove repeated plots or tones.
9. Record the approved version and the important revision decisions. Do not label an exploratory draft as final.
